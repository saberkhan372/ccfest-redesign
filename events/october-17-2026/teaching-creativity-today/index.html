CC Fest’s first panel asked how to teach creativity through creative coding when AI can make almost anything. Amy B. Woodman, senior program manager at the Processing Foundation and a former classroom teacher, brought together three colleagues: Daniel Schneider, a former Tucson high school math and computer science teacher who has written middle school and AI curricula for Code.org and now Hello World CS; David DeLiema, a learning scientist at the University of Minnesota who studies failure and play in learning; and Adrienne Gifford, in her 21st year teaching middle school computing and STEM and a collaborator with the University of Washington’s Center for Learning, Computing, and Imagination. They had written questions for each other in advance without sharing answers, so the conversation unfolded like a podcast.

Woodman opened with her worry: making is how we learn to think, discover our tastes and preferences, learn to judge, and learn to express ourselves. If AI makes things for us, she asked, what happens to that? Schneider said that rooms where standards and policies are written now focus on technical skills, AI careers and even staying ahead of other countries, and that this narrative leaves out students’ own desire to create. He argued that if self-expression is the goal, AI complicates the path but does not change the goal, and offered a counterpoint: his 70-year-old father used AI to finally build the quirky website no consultant understood.

DeLiema described debugging as full of open-ended, creative choices: what counts as wrong, what to blame, how to intervene, and what you want to learn from the moment. “There’s humor, there’s joy, there’s empathy. It’s human interaction. It’s human connection,” he said of the conversations around broken code. He described students who sang to each other when something failed and worried about handing that social process to a tool. Pointing to Courtney Cazden’s research on prompts, he noted that a hint only works if you already have the knowledge it cues, which is why learners have to own their learning process.

Gifford reported that her seventh graders made identity-based pixel art in Python with an optional AI agent, and almost none chose to use it. In reflections, they said they wanted their work to be authentic, that their ideas were better than AI’s, that they preferred help from teachers and peers, and that they had concerns about artists’ work and water use. Twelve-year-olds, she said, are asking the same questions as artists and researchers, and students should be able to question, change or reject the tools. What they want protected is agency over their creative process, not speed. She suggested next year’s panel feature students.

Woodman described the Art + Code curriculum for art teachers, guided by Seymour Papert’s idea that educators create conditions for invention rather than provide ready-made knowledge. Tutorials such as Build a World and a shared Drawing Tool give learners challenges just out of reach, results that are clearly their own, and communal moments where they use each other’s work. She also cited the balance of order and chaos in Aleksandra Jovanić’s existing Take a Spline for a Walk tutorial, connecting constraints in a drawing system to the judgment a maker develops. Saber Khan closed by urging teachers to make their classrooms places where skeptical voices are heard.

Three takeaways

  • Ask the students. Gifford’s seventh graders mostly declined an optional AI helper, citing authenticity, their own ideas, human help and ethics (00:14:58–00:18:28).
  • Debugging is creative and social. Noticing, blaming and fixing are open-ended choices, and the talk around them builds connection (00:10:00–00:13:50).
  • Design challenges just out of reach. Papert’s “conditions for invention” guided tutorials where every learner’s result is different and shared (00:19:24–00:24:52).

Try it

Editorial suggestion, adapted from Gifford’s classroom: on your next creative coding project, make an AI helper optional and ask students to write a short reflection on whether and why they used it. Share what they say with them, and with colleagues.
